Roof Decking: What Happens When the Old Roof Comes Off

By Chris Jackson, Owner · September 16, 2026

Roof decking is the layer of wood your shingles are nailed to, and nobody sees it until the old roof comes off. That is why a good quote can tell you how decking will be handled, but cannot honestly tell you how much of it needs replacing before the tear off starts.

What roof decking actually is

Under every shingle roof is a solid layer of wood fastened across the rafters. On most North Texas homes built in the last few decades, that layer is plywood or OSB, the panel made from pressed wood strands. Older homes sometimes have board decking instead: long planks laid side by side, often with small gaps between them. The underlayment goes on top of the decking, and the shingles go on top of that.

The decking is what the roofing nails actually grip. Shingles stay down in the wind because every nail bites into sound wood. If the wood underneath is soft, rotted or coming apart, those nails have nothing to hold, and a brand new roof can start lifting or leaking far sooner than it should. That is the whole reason decking matters during a roof replacement, even though you will never see it again once the job is done.

Why nobody can count bad sheets before the tear off

From the yard, and even from the attic, you only see part of the story. Shingles hide the top face of the deck completely. From the attic you can see the underside, and that is worth a look: dark water stains, rusty nail tips or sheets that sag between rafters all point at trouble. But a stain underneath does not tell you how far the damage runs, and plenty of soft wood looks perfectly fine from below.

So an honest roofer does not promise a number of sheets in advance. Anyone who does is guessing, and a low guess only turns into a surprise halfway through the job. What a quote can and should do is explain how decking will be handled: that the crew inspects the deck once the old roof is off, that you will be told what they found and why, and how any replacement sheets are counted. Ask about that before you sign anything. It belongs on the same list as the questions in how to choose a roofer without getting burned.

What a crew looks for once the deck is exposed

With the shingles and old underlayment stripped off, the deck is right there under foot. This is the one moment in the life of a roof when every sheet can be checked. These are the usual reasons a section gets replaced:

  • Soft or spongy spots that give when someone steps on them, usually from a slow leak that went on for years.
  • Rot or dark staining around the places water likes to get in: valleys, chimneys, skylights, pipe boots and the low edges of the roof.
  • Panels coming apart, where plywood layers or OSB strands have started separating after soaking and drying out over and over.
  • Wood chewed up by old nails from earlier roofs, so a new nail has nothing solid to bite.
  • Board decking with wide gaps that shingles cannot sit on properly, which may need new sheathing laid over it first.

Bad sections get cut out and replaced with new panels fastened to the rafters, and the new underlayment and shingles go down over sound wood. On most jobs this happens as part of the same tear off, before anything new is installed, so the roof is not left open any longer than it has to be. What damages decking in North Texas

Decking rarely fails on its own. It fails because water or trapped heat got to it. The most common cause is a leak nobody caught: a lifted shingle, a cracked pipe boot or tired flashing that let a little water in with every rain for a long time. By the time a stain shows up on a ceiling, the wood above it has usually been wet for a while.

A stuffy attic is the second cause. When an attic cannot breathe, warm damp air collects under the deck, and over the years that can warp and weaken the wood from below. We wrote more about that in attic ventilation and roof lifespan.

Hail is the third, and in this part of Texas it is not a small one. The NOAA Storm Events Database logged 137 hail reports across Collin, Denton, Dallas, Rockwall and Kaufman counties between June 2024 and May 2026, spread over 27 separate storm days. 122 of those reports were hail one inch or larger, and the biggest was 3.0 inch hail near Lakeview in Dallas County on April 25, 2026. Hail does not usually break decking directly. What it does is crack and bruise shingles, and a damaged shingle that never gets fixed becomes the slow leak that rots the wood underneath. What you can check yourself, safely

You do not need to climb onto the roof to learn something useful, and we would rather you stayed off it. Two checks from inside the house tell you plenty.

First, take a flashlight into the attic on a dry day and look at the underside of the deck, especially around chimneys, vents and the low edges of the roof. Water stains, dark rings and rusted nail points are all worth noting. Second, look over the ceilings below for brown stains or bubbling paint. Neither check tells you how many sheets need replacing, but both tell a roofer where to look first, so mention anything you find when someone comes out.

Frequently asked questions

Does every roof replacement need new decking?

No. Many roofs come off with a deck that is sound from edge to edge, and nothing needs replacing. Others need a few sheets around an old leak. It depends on the history of that particular roof, which is exactly why the deck is checked after the tear off rather than assumed.

Can decking be replaced without replacing the roof?

Only by taking off the roofing above it. A small section can sometimes be handled during a repair, but the shingles and underlayment over the bad wood have to come off either way, because there is no way to reach the deck from on top of them.

Is OSB worse than plywood?

Both are standard decking materials and both hold up well when they stay dry. OSB tends to swell at the edges when it gets wet again and again, while plywood tends to separate into layers. In the end they fail for the same reason: water they were never meant to hold.

Should I worry if my home has board decking?

Not by itself. Plenty of older homes have it. The crew checks whether the boards are sound and whether the gaps between them are tight enough for shingles, and covers them with new sheathing where they are not.
