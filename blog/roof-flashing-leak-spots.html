The Five Places a Roof Usually Leaks First

By Chris Jackson, Owner · September 13, 2026

A roof almost never leaks through the middle of a shingle. That part of the roof is doing exactly what it was designed to do. Water gets in where the roof stops being a plain slope and has to turn a corner, wrap around something, or meet a wall.

After twenty six years on North Texas roofs, the leaks we get called out to land in the same five places over and over. Knowing which five they are will not fix your roof, but it will tell you whether the stain on your ceiling is a small job or a serious one, and it will stop you paying somebody to chase a leak in the wrong place.

Why roofs leak at the details, not the field

The open area of a roof slope is called the field, and it is the simple part. Shingles overlap, water runs down the overlaps and off the edge, and gravity does the rest.

Everywhere else, somebody had to make a decision. Where two slopes meet, where a pipe comes through, where the roof runs into a brick wall, a detail had to be built to keep water out. Those details rely on metal, sealant and correct layering rather than on gravity alone, and they are the parts that age, move and fail.

Heat drives most of that movement here. Metal expands and contracts through every North Texas summer day, and sealant that was flexible when it went on goes hard and splits after enough cycles. We cover what that heat does to the rest of the roof in why Texas heat ages roofs faster.

1. Step flashing where the roof meets a wall

This is the most common leak we find, and the one most often patched badly.

Where a roof slope runs into a vertical wall, such as the side of a dormer or a second storey, the waterproofing is not one long strip of metal. It should be a series of small L shaped pieces, one woven in with each course of shingles, so water stepping down the slope is always handed onto the piece below it.

When somebody replaces that with a single run of metal and a bead of sealant, it works until the sealant cracks. Then water runs behind the metal and straight into the wall. The tell inside is a stain on an upstairs ceiling close to an exterior wall, or a damp patch appearing high on the wall itself rather than in the middle of a room.

2. Valleys where two slopes meet

A valley carries more water than any other part of the roof, because it collects everything off two slopes and funnels it into one channel.

That volume is why valleys fail. The shingles or metal lining the valley take the most water, the most grit and the most wear, and any gap in the layering gives water a direct route under the roof. Debris makes it worse. Leaves and grit packing into a valley dam the channel and push water sideways under the shingles instead of down.

From the ground, look along each valley line for shingles that look worn, lifted or discoloured compared with the slope either side, and for anything visibly collected in the channel.

3. Pipe boots and roof penetrations

Every plumbing vent, exhaust and skylight is a deliberate hole in your roof with a cover over it. The cover on a plumbing vent is usually a rubber collar called a boot.

Rubber is the shortest lived thing on a roof in this climate. It sits in direct sun, it bakes, it goes brittle and it splits, usually around the base of the pipe where it is stretched tightest. We see split boots on roofs that still have years of shingle life left in them, and it is one of the cheapest repairs in roofing if you catch it.

Inside, a boot leak tends to show up as a small, well defined stain that appears in the same spot every time it rains hard, often above a bathroom or a kitchen, because that is where the plumbing vents run.

4. Chimney flashing

A chimney is a large obstruction sitting in the path of everything running down the slope above it, and it needs flashing on all four sides to deal with that.

The back side is the one that fails. Water coming down the slope hits the back of the chimney and has to be diverted around it, which is what a cricket, a small ridged structure behind the chimney, is for. Plenty of homes never got one. Without it, water and debris sit against the brick until something gives.

The masonry itself can also let water in through cracked mortar or a damaged crown, which is a separate trade from roofing. Part of an honest inspection is telling you which of the two you are actually dealing with.

5. Where the roof meets a lower roof or a porch

Any place a slope ties into a lower section, over a porch, an addition or a bay window, water has to change direction and the layering has to be right.

These tie-ins are often where an addition was joined to the original house, sometimes years apart and sometimes by different hands. The transition is the weak point, and on the low pitch sections common over porches, water moves slowly enough to find any gap it is offered.

The stain from one of these usually appears on a ceiling directly below the tie-in, and it often gets blamed on a window instead of the roof.

What hail does to all five of these

Every one of these details is metal, rubber or sealant, and every one of them is more exposed than the shingles around it.

The NOAA Storm Events Database logged 137 hail reports across Collin, Denton, Dallas, Rockwall and Kaufman counties between June 2024 and May 2026, on 27 separate storm days, and 122 of those reports involved stones an inch across or larger. A photo posted on 25 April 2026 showed hail estimated at up to 3 inches around Dallas Baptist University, and a spotter report on 27 April 2026 described hail in Fate large enough to leave holes in metal.

Stones like that dent flashing, crack aged sealant and split rubber boots that were already close to done. A roof can come through a storm with its shingles broadly intact and still have two or three compromised details, which is why a leak sometimes shows up weeks after the storm rather than the same night. The full picture is in the signs of hail damage on a roof.

How to find the source from inside

Water does not fall straight down. It runs along the top of the decking, then along a rafter, and it drips at the lowest point it reaches, which can be several feet from where it came in.

So the stain tells you roughly where to start looking, not where the hole is. What helps is noting which storms produce it. A leak that only appears in wind driven rain from one direction usually points at a wall or chimney detail on that side. A leak that shows up in any hard rain is more likely a valley or a penetration. A stain that grows slowly with no rain at all is often condensation or plumbing, not the roof.

Please do this from inside or from the ground. A composition roof is slick and steep enough to be genuinely dangerous, and no leak is worth it. Our roof maintenance checklist for Texas homeowners covers what you can safely check yourself.

Repair or replace

Most of these are repairs, and we would rather tell you that than sell you something bigger.

A split boot, a length of step flashing, a valley reworked properly, these are all jobs on an otherwise sound roof. The answer changes when the shingles around the detail are already at the end of their life, or when water has been getting in long enough to soften the decking underneath. At that point you are not fixing a detail, you are working around a roof that is finished.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the ceiling stain not directly under the hole?

Because water runs along the decking and down a rafter before it drips. It leaves the roof at the lowest point it can reach, which is often several feet from where it got in. That is the main reason leak chasing goes wrong.

How long does a rubber pipe boot last in Texas?

Less time than the shingles around it. Direct sun and heat cycling make rubber brittle, and a split boot on a roof with plenty of shingle life left is one of the most common repairs we do.

Can a roof leak without any missing shingles?

Easily. Most leaks start at flashing, valleys and penetrations rather than in the open field of the roof, so a roof can look perfectly intact from the street and still be letting water in at a detail.

My leak only shows up in some storms. Why?

That usually means wind direction matters, which points to a vertical detail such as a wall or chimney on one side of the house. Leaks that appear in any heavy rain are more often valleys or penetrations.
